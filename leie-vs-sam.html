LEIE vs SAM.gov: the two federal exclusion lists, compared

who publishes each · what an entry means · what neither covers

Every exclusion-screening rule in health care names two federal databases: the OIG's List of Excluded Individuals and Entities (LEIE) and the System for Award Management (SAM.gov). They are often spoken of as one check. They are not: different agencies, different legal bases, different scope, different fields, different schedules. Here is what each one is, how they overlap, and what falls between them.

How they overlap

OIG reports its exclusions to SAM.gov, so an LEIE entry usually has a SAM counterpart naming "HHS-OIG" as the excluding agency. The reverse is far from true: SAM holds roughly twice as many active exclusion records as the LEIE, most of them from agencies that have nothing to do with health care. Checking SAM alone therefore finds more people but with far more noise for a provider roster; checking the LEIE alone misses exclusions that other agencies imposed. The two files also update on different cycles and publish different identifiers, which is why 42 CFR 455.436 asks state Medicaid programs to check both every month rather than either one.

In RosterProof the two are loaded as separate sources (SOURCE_ID = 'LEIE' and 'SAM') with EXCLUSION_AUTHORITY carrying the excluding agency on SAM records, so a screen can treat them as two checks while running them as one.

What neither one covers

State Medicaid agencies exclude and terminate providers on their own authority: license actions, program-integrity findings, Medicaid fraud-unit referrals, terminations for cause. Those actions are supposed to flow to OIG, and many do, but across every state list RosterProof loads about half of the active records have no LEIE counterpart, and in some states more than 90% (the federal gap, measured per state). Under ACA §6501 a termination for cause in one state is grounds for termination in every other, so these records matter everywhere. The state lists are the third check, and the one most often skipped.

Drug and device sponsors have a fourth and fifth: the FDA debarment list and the clinical-investigator disqualification list (life sciences screening). And a Medicare opt-out affidavit, which some screening tools surface next to exclusions, is a status rather than a sanction and should never be treated as a hit.
